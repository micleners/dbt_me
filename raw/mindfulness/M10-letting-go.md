# M10: Letting Go of Judgments

## Introduction

Building on the previous exercise on mindful awareness of negative judgments, this lesson teaches techniques to release counterproductive thoughts. "The mere act of observing and noticing judgments helped you adjust to some of those judgments." This exercise guides practitioners through objectively rewriting judgmental thoughts in more constructive forms.

Practice both exercises daily for one week.

## Judgment into Non-Judgment

This exercise involves reframing negative judgments into non-judgmental observations. Describe situations factually, acknowledge your feelings, and note your thoughts—all without judgmental language.

### Examples:

**Example 1**
- Judgment: "I hate this baby crying on the plane. Not everyone has to be a parent!"
- Mindful form: "I hear a baby crying loudly in the seat behind. I feel angry at his parents and I feel like they need to do a better job. I am feeling irritated and angry."

**Example 2**
- Judgment: "I can't seem to advance in my career, I am such a weak person. Probably nobody can even remember my name because I am unimpressive."
- Mindful form: "I haven't had a promotion in five years now, and that makes me want to blame myself. I am feeling discouraged. I am also thinking that maybe the reason I didn't get the promotion is that I am not personable enough."

**Example 3**
- Judgment: "My boyfriend is so selfish, I hate him! What a jerk! He only thinks about himself! He always forgets the plans we make."
- Mindful form: "My boyfriend forgot about the plan we made today. He doesn't always do that though. I feel angry and I feel like I want to have an argument with him. I am thinking that he only thinks about himself (but that is not true)."

**Example 4**
- Judgment: "I can't stand going to the family gatherings. My in-laws are so mean. They are always bragging and they are so fake!"
- Mindful form: "My in-laws make comments that annoy me. I don't like how much they talk about buying stuff. They make me feel unfriendly and like going home."

**Example 5**
- Judgment: "The stupid driver that was in front me obviously didn't know how to drive! Who does he think he is, cutting me off!"
- Mindful form: "The person in front of me was driving very fast and cut across my lane. I felt unsafe. This whole situation made me feel enraged."

Complete the provided worksheet with your own examples.

## Repetitive Judgments

This exercise addresses recurring negative judgments about yourself, events, or others.

### Step One: Describe the Judgment

Write the judgment objectively and non-judgmentally, as though observing from outside. State only facts. Example: "I have been smoking for 2 years now, and I usually tend to label myself with offensive words because I haven't managed to quit yet. I have this bad habit and I know that it is harmful for my health."

### Step Two: How I Feel Because of the Judgment

Document how the judgment affects your emotional state. Does it worsen or improve your feelings? Example: "After I judge myself I start feeling self-hatred and I feel depressed. That just makes me want to smoke more so that I'll soothe myself, and I have even less confidence that one day I will quit."

### Step Three: What Life Would Look Like Without this Judgment

Imagine life without this negative judgment. How would you feel differently? What different actions might you take? What alternative results might occur?

Example: "If I didn't have this negative judgment about my smoking habit I would probably feel less self-hatred and depression. I think that I would accept faster and more fully that I have a bad habit that needs changing. I wouldn't feel burdened with all these negative emotions caused by judging myself, and maybe I would try a program or read a book that would help me quit."

### Practice

Throughout each day, when this judgment arises, transform it using non-judgmental language. Rather than reinforcing the judgment, remind yourself that dwelling judgmentally serves no purpose. Notice and accept the shift toward non-judgmental awareness. Gradually, this practice may help you move toward finding practical solutions.

## FAQs

**Q: Must I choose a repeatedly occurring judgment?**

A: No. A recent judgment works equally well. "If you remembered it, then it is perhaps one that bothers you and that you would want to mindfully look at." Practicing with any judgment increases your awareness for future work.

**Q: How do I verify I'm executing Part 1 correctly?**

A: Use phrases like "I am thinking," "I have the feeling of," or "I want to act in (a certain way)." Describe situations [as though you observed the situation from the outside](https://dialecticalbehaviortherapy.com/mindfulness/observing/). State only facts objectively. If reading your mindful version still triggers negative emotions, your language may retain judgment. Review the [Mental Noting](https://dialecticalbehaviortherapy.com/mindfulness/mental-noting/) exercise for additional support.

**Q: Are judgmental thoughts really problematic?**

A: Without mindfulness practice, judgment's emotional impact often goes unnoticed. Review [Mindfulness of Negative Judgments](https://dialecticalbehaviortherapy.com/mindfulness/mindfulness-of-negative-judgments/) first and practice for a week. "With time you will probably become aware of emotions that you previously weren't noticing."
