# M5: Mental Noting

## Introduction

Our thinking mind frequently creates distractions in daily life, generating numerous thoughts—some neutral, some useful, and many automatically negative. These unconscious worrying thoughts often result in anxiety, depression, overwhelming emotions, and disconnection from yourself and others.

Mental noting helps you become more aware of your thoughts and emotions, reducing the likelihood of getting caught up in them. This exercise has two levels:

- **Level 1**: Ideal for beginners. Purpose: Noting your thoughts.
- **Level 2**: Intermediate. Purpose: Noting all experiences (thoughts, emotions, physical sensations, distracting or upsetting events).

## Mental Noting Thoughts

### Level 1: Noting Your Thoughts

1. Set a timer for 5 minutes
2. Use the Level 1 section on the worksheet
3. Structure observations as: "I am thinking about X"

Mental noting means describing thoughts without trying to control them—you are simply observing. Find a comfortable, undisturbed place and tune into your thought content. As you become aware of thoughts, write them down briefly without analysis. Shift focus to your next thought.

**Examples:**
- I am thinking about how my work day went.
- I am thinking about my dog.
- I am thinking about the movie that I saw yesterday.
- I am thinking about the plot of the book that I'm reading.

## Mental Noting Experiences

### Level 2: Noting Your Experiences

1. Set a timer for 10 minutes
2. Use the Level 2 section on the worksheet
3. Structure observations using these formats:
   - I am thinking about X.
   - I feel the emotion Y.
   - When I think about X, the emotion Y occurs in me.
   - When the event X happens, I feel Y and my thoughts are Z.

Note your thoughts, emotions, observations, and physical sensations without analyzing or judging. Describe experiences as an objective observer.

**Instead of:** "I can't believe how depressed I feel! Another day where I won't be able to do anything useful."
**Try:** "I can notice sadness and depressed mood inside me. I also feel unmotivated and tired."

**Instead of:** "I don't know how I'm going to make ends meet this month. Everything is just going to be worse."
**Try:** "I am thinking about not having enough money. I feel hopeless and keep thinking negative things about the future."

By describing experiences rather than analyzing them, you remove unnecessary suffering layers. Mental noting brings clarity and calmness to situations.

**Additional Examples:**
- I feel nervous when I think about finding a parking space tomorrow.
- When I'm having an argument with my boyfriend (event), I feel extremely angry.
- I am thinking about how hard it is for me to meet new people, and that makes me feel sad.
- I feel irritable, and that makes me feel like arguing with my family.
- After my retirement (event), I feel very lonely.

## FAQs

**Q: It's my first time and I find this difficult. How can I make it easier?**

As with all DBT techniques, mental noting may require practice before feeling comfortable. Maintain a light attitude and gently bring awareness to your thoughts. Write down the first thing that comes to mind, then do this with each new thought. It becomes easier with practice.

**Q: If I have an objective problem, how will mental noting solve it?**

Mental noting observes our outlook on events inside and outside ourselves. Objective problems and negative feelings are part of human experience. By being less reactive to negative experiences, you gain clarity to see solutions. Mental noting helps transform problems from negative situations into events that are currently happening and can be changed.

**Q: I feel like I'm writing monotonously.**

Don't view this as a chore—you're doing it for your well-being. By practicing this mindfulness exercise, you calm distracting thoughts and emotions while gaining clarity on how to react in situations. When you observe a thought without labeling it, that thought has less impact and directs your behavior less.
