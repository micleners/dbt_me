# Willingness vs Willfulness: Full Article Content

## Introduction

In DBT, understanding willingness and willfulness is crucial for behavioral change and acceptance.

**Willingness** refers to "the ability to be open, receptive, and engaged in the present moment." It involves embracing acceptance and participating fully in the change process. This means being open to new experiences and strategies that improve well-being, recognizing reality, and solving problems effectively. Willingness is the opposite of resistance—fighting what's happening and refusing to tolerate facts.

**Willfulness** represents "a state of stubbornness or resistance to change." It involves rigid, inflexible thinking and refusing to consider alternative approaches. While popular culture celebrates grit and willpower, DBT views willfulness as a limiting perspective. It manifests as unwillingness to try new coping skills, reluctance to address problems, fear of change, desire for control, or belief that current strategies are the only valid ones.

The key insight: some situations cannot or shouldn't be changed. Instead, we must recognize reality and be flexible in problem-solving. By cultivating willingness, you approach challenging situations with an open mind and full engagement, leading to greater self-awareness, emotional regulation, and personal growth.

## Instructions

This three-step exercise helps solve problematic situations by distinguishing between fighting reality and flexibly addressing it.

### Step One: Describe the Situation

Describe the problematic situation by stating facts only—what happened, when, who was involved, and what the problem is. Avoid including feelings or thoughts; focus purely on observable events. This objective approach helps you see the situation in a way that enables solving it willingly rather than willfully through resistance.

**Example:** "I got a bad performance review at work last week. My supervisor and I don't see eye to eye. I am very dissatisfied and don't want to accept the performance review."

### Step Two: How Are You Willful About This Situation

Describe emotions, thoughts, and physical sensations reflecting your resistance to what's happening. Then write the result of this resistance approach—what you want to do.

**Example:** "I really don't believe that I was treated fairly, and I think that I deserve a much better performance review and raise than what I got. All of my other performance reviews have been great, so I don't think that this is right! I assume that my new supervisor has it out for me, and doesn't appreciate all the work I am doing because I do my work quietly and suck-up. Also, I'm being punished for disagreeing, I don't just nod yes to everything like my coworkers. This is just so unfair!

I want to talk to my supervisor and HR and really just have an argument about this performance review. I am feeling very angry and I see no problem in me expressing that anger!"

*This approach produces tension and isn't optimal. A heated argument likely won't yield positive results.*

### Step Three: What Are You Willing To Do

Explore the alternative of willingness. If you accepted all facts of the situation, what emotions and thoughts would arise? What flexible, alternative solutions exist versus fighting unchangeable facts?

**Example:** "If I accept the facts of the situation, that I got a bad performance review and that right now I can't change the past or the assessment that I got, I would probably feel a little ashamed but also really curious about what went wrong. What I would do is have a meeting with my supervisor, and openly try to understand what my weaknesses were. Maybe there is really something that I haven't understood well, or maybe I've missed something important. Either way, there is no harm in asking in a calm manner. I will proactively ask for more frequent feedback. If none of that works and I just can't get on with them, maybe I will just explain that we aren't a good fit and see if I can get transferred to another division or look for another job."

Willingness can feel abstract initially. Consider how you'd apply this approach in everyday life. Notice when you refuse to accept unchangeable situations, and practice substituting that resistance with a more liberating willingness attitude.

## FAQs

**Is this concept similar to radical acceptance?**

Many DBT skills connect to one another. Radical acceptance specifically focuses on "accepting reality as it is without judgment or resistance." To be willing to find flexible solutions to problematic situations, you first need to radically accept the facts. Additionally, consulting your wise mind helps verify that your chosen solution feels right.

**How can I more easily remember the difference between willfulness and willingness?**

Remember it this way: if you feel frustrated trying to change something unchangeable while expending energy in vain—that's willfulness. Conversely, willingness makes you more effective at problem-solving. You accept facts and do what's necessary—no more, no less. For most people, willingness feels lighter and more liberating than willfulness.

**How does this exercise fit into the big picture of distress tolerance?**

This exercise develops new problem-solving skills. If overwhelming emotions prevent optimal living, this exercise proves useful. "Thinking about events from the perspective of willingness will soothe your extreme emotions and will help you in a situation of emotional distress." These distress tolerance skills help survive emotional crises without worsening them.
