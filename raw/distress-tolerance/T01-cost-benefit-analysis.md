# Cost Benefit Analysis: DBT Distress Tolerance Exercise

## Introduction

This DBT exercise addresses mixed motivations when engaging in behaviors that may be harmful. As the resource explains, "People often have mixed motivations when they enjoy a certain behavior that may not be good for them." The Cost Benefit Analysis helps individuals examine both sides of problematic behaviors and their healthier alternatives to build motivation for change.

**Purpose:** "Cost Benefit Analysis helps you decide whether a problematic behavior is worth changing"

## When to Use

Complete this exercise when considering changing a problematic behavior. It provides leverage for maintaining behavioral changes by creating concrete evidence of why modification is necessary.

## Step-by-Step Instructions

**Step One: Choose a Behavior**
Identify habits in your life that aren't serving you well. Common examples include smoking, excessive eating, impulsive spending, excessive television watching, or alcohol overuse. Select one habit you most want to change.

**Step Two: Cost of Engaging in Problematic Behavior**
"Write down every negative consequence that results from you doing the bad habit." Brainstorm thoroughly, starting with obvious consequences and extending your list comprehensively.

**Step Three: Benefits of Engaging in the Problematic Behavior**
List reasons you engage in this behavior. Note that these "benefits" typically provide short-term rewards with long-term negative consequences.

**Step Four: Costs of Engaging in an Alternative Behavior**
Identify all negative aspects of changing your behavior. "Changing behaviors requires effort and energy, paying close attention to what you choose to do." Consider discomfort, effort required, and adjustment challenges.

**Step Five: Benefits of Engaging in an Alternative Behavior**
Visualize and list possible benefits from behavioral change. "Really try to imagine the feeling of these benefits! Feel how much more energy you'll have, the long-term satisfaction, and fulfillment you'll feel!"

**Step Six: Score Costs and Benefits**
Assign importance ratings (1-5 scale, with 5 being extremely important) to each cost and benefit. This numerical approach reveals that "the costs of engaging in the problematic behavior and the benefits of doing the alternative action are much more important."

## Example: Eating Too Much Junk Food

**Problematic Behavior Costs:**
- Lacks nutrients and healthy ingredients (5)
- Energy crashes after meals (4)
- Weight gain and appearance dissatisfaction (5)
- More frequent illness (4)
- Addictive patterns leading to overeating (3)
- Guilt and feelings of lost self-control (4)

**Problematic Behavior Benefits:**
- Tastes good (4)
- Fast and requires no cooking (4)
- Immediate satisfaction (3)
- Convenient when away from home (2)
- Many options available (2)
- Provides emotional comfort when sad/depressed (3)

**Alternative Behavior (Eating Healthy) Costs:**
- Doesn't taste as good initially (3)
- Requires more cooking (3)
- Difficult to maintain consistency (4)
- Concern about appearing pretentious socially (2)
- Need alternative stress management (3)

**Alternative Behavior Benefits:**
- Significantly increased energy (5)
- Better health and disease prevention (5)
- Improved appearance and weight loss (4)
- No energy crashes (4)
- Better mood and depression relief (5)
- Improved sleep quality (4)

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What if I still choose the bad habit after completing this exercise?**

"Changing a habit is not an easy task, it is a gradual process." Success requires daily commitment and regular review of your list. "Be persistent, and willing to work for your change!" Consider adding new costs and benefits as you gain awareness.

**How can I be sure the benefits of alternative behavior will feel real?**

If you haven't attempted the new behavior, uncertainty is natural. "That is why you can start by trying out a new action and test it out." Trial periods help you experience whether changes truly produce the benefits you anticipate.

**I can't think of many costs or benefits—what should I do?**

"If you do this exercise for the first time, it takes a little time to get used to the format." Consider how the behavior affects different life areas. Research unknown consequences of specific behaviors like smoking or alcohol use to expand your awareness.
